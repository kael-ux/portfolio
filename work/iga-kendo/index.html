The four things everyone asks first

Nobody emails a dojo to ask basic questions. So here they are, answered before you have to.

01

Do I need equipment?

No. Beginners train in a t-shirt and jogging pants and borrow a shinai. Armour comes months later, once your basics hold up.

02

Do I need to be fit?

No. Kendo builds the conditioning as you go. Our members range from students to doctors to people in their sixties.

03

What does it cost?

Monthly dues, plus a one-off federation registration. No lock-in contract. Ask at any session and they will walk you through it.

04

When can I start?

Beginner batches open a few times a year. Join the waitlist and we'll message you the moment the next one opens.

Our history

Taught properly, or not at all

“Do not focus on winning. Just do beautiful kendo.”

— Koji Igarashi, 6th Dan, founder

Koji Igarashi-sensei, 6th Dan Founder. Taught here from the club’s beginning until his death in 2025.

Igarashi-sensei was born in Tokyo and began kendo at thirteen, under Ikuo Suzuki (8th Dan) at Chushinkan Dojo. He carried a dream of one day building a dojo and sharing the way of the sword beyond Japan. Work eventually brought him to the Philippines, where he found a small kendo community and began practising with them.

When he had the chance to stay longer, he started teaching kendo to children in Makati City. With his wife Junko Igarashi he established Herald Kendo Club — Hera-Ken. He later met the Filipino kendoka Kristopher “Kutch” Inting, and together they built a kendo community among students at the University of the Philippines Diliman. From that effort, IGA Kendo Club was established in 2010.

His teaching emphasised kihon — fundamentals, proper form, strong spirit. He told students that a good offense leads to good defense: that moving forward with conviction beats retreating or merely blocking. Koji Igarashi passed away in 2025. The spirit of his teaching still guides the dojo and the community he helped build.

The club is led today by Head Sensei Kristopher Inting — the first homegrown Filipino kendoka to reach 5th Dan — together with Senseis Loida Inting, Fides Desacada and Joshua Espiritu. Sensei Keitai Shingaki remains closely affiliated, sharing his guidance and instruction with members.

Members come from many different walks of life, united by a shared passion for kendo. The club welcomes practitioners of all ages — from young children to adults — so beginners and experienced kendoka train together and learn from one another.

What has not changed is the foundation: sincere practice, strong fundamentals, and the pursuit of beautiful kendo — just as Igarashi-sensei taught.

Our logo

Eight rays, seven virtues

A kamon (家紋) is a Japanese family crest — 家 ka, family, and 紋 mon, mark. Ours is a sun, referencing the suns of both the Philippine and Japanese flags, and the members’ own rise toward becoming a better person and kendoka.

The intertwining lines form eight rays. On the Philippine flag those eight rays stand for eight provinces; here they stand for the seven virtues of Bushido — plus the club’s motto as the eighth. They intertwine because the virtues and the motto cannot exist without one another.

義Gi — integrity, justice
礼Rei — respect
勇Yu — courage
名誉Meiyo — honour
仁Jin — compassion
誠Makoto — honesty, sincerity
忠義Chu — duty, loyalty
八Enjoy Kendo!

The circle depicts the wholeness of every element inside it — totality with no beginning and no end, and so infinite. The blue denotes bravery and dedication. It stands for the introspective journey, and symbolises wisdom and depth of understanding, peaceful rest, profound insight, spiritual realisation.

Kamon design and conceptualisation by Rikki Daniel Reyes — “Rikki-kun” — Art Director and senior member of the club.

Competition

We go out under the flag

Members grade and compete under the United Kendo Federation of the Philippines — at the national tournament and abroad, against the rest of Southeast Asia. Nobody is required to compete. Plenty do.

Philippine team at international competition. The Philippines sits with the other national federations at the delegation table — grading and results are recognised by the International Kendo Federation.
